How platform teams use resource, access, service-consumption, placement, and availability boundaries to turn Supervisor into a governed internal platform.

01 · Why another boundary is needed

Kubernetes namespaces organise application resources. Platform teams must govern the infrastructure underneath them.

Kubernetes namespaces provide a scope for namespaced objects such as Deployments and Services. They can also be combined with RBAC and ResourceQuota to divide cluster resources among teams.

But the platform team has a wider problem. Before an application team can create a Kubernetes namespace, someone must decide which infrastructure it may consume, how much capacity it may use, which storage policies and VM Classes are available, who has access, and which failure domains may host the workload.

Memory hook

A Kubernetes namespace organises application resources inside one Kubernetes cluster. A vSphere Namespace governs access to infrastructure and platform capabilities delivered through Supervisor.

Important boundary

A namespace by itself is not proof of strong tenant isolation. Effective isolation depends on identity, RBAC, resource controls, networking, storage, workload security, and operational policy.

02 · The platform consumption boundary

A vSphere Namespace brings resource, access, service, and placement decisions together.

A vSphere Namespace is a governed boundary on Supervisor. It is where administrators assign platform capacity, access, services, and placement options for workloads such as VKS clusters, VM Service virtual machines, vSphere Pods, and Supervisor Services.

Resource boundary

Defines the infrastructure capacity available to workloads created through the namespace.

Access boundary

Controls which users and groups can consume or administer platform capabilities.

Service boundary

Publishes approved storage policies, VM Classes, networks, content, and services.

Placement boundary

Maps the namespace to one or more eligible vSphere Zones.

One-sentence definition: A vSphere Namespace is the governed platform boundary through which teams consume Supervisor infrastructure and services.

04 · What the platform team assigns

The namespace publishes approved capabilities rather than exposing the full infrastructure estate.

  • CPU and memory capacity: platform-level resource controls bound what workloads created through the namespace may consume.
  • Storage policies: expose approved storage capabilities that workloads later consume through Kubernetes storage constructs.
  • VM Classes: publish approved virtual-machine shapes and capabilities for VKS nodes and VM Service workloads.
  • Content and images: make approved content libraries or images available where required.
  • Networking: expose the network model associated with the namespace, depending on the Supervisor architecture.
  • Users and groups: define who may inspect, consume, or administer resources through the namespace.
  • Zone mappings: define which infrastructure placement domains are available.
Storage memory hook

The platform team publishes the approved storage menu. The application team requests storage from that menu.

05 · Tenancy patterns

Choose namespace boundaries according to ownership, policy, lifecycle, and risk.

One namespace per application

Best when an application needs independent capacity, storage, access, and lifecycle controls.

Trade-off: stronger separation, more namespaces to operate.

One namespace per team

Works when several related applications share owners, policies, and lifecycle.

Trade-off: easier administration, but workloads share quota and access boundaries.

One namespace per environment

Separates development, test, and production with different quotas, policies, and permissions.

Trade-off: more objects, clearer production governance.

Shared namespace

Suitable for small non-production environments or tightly related workloads.

Trade-off: lower overhead, larger shared blast radius.

Decision rule: Share a namespace only when workloads genuinely have the same owners, access model, capacity policy, storage and network requirements, availability needs, and lifecycle expectations.

06 · vSphere Zones

A zone represents an infrastructure placement and failure-domain choice.

A vSphere Zone is a logical grouping of vSphere infrastructure used to represent placement and failure-domain choices for Supervisor and workloads. In common designs, a zone maps to a vSphere cluster.

Availability

Distribute control-plane components or workloads across independent infrastructure domains.

Isolation

Separate management resources from workloads or dedicate specialised infrastructure.

Specialisation

Publish GPU, high-performance, or regulated infrastructure selectively.

Placement

Limit where workloads created through a namespace are eligible to run.

Do not overstate a zone

A vSphere Zone is not automatically a complete geographic disaster-recovery site. Its resilience depends on the physical power, network, storage, rack, room, and site design underneath it.

08 · Namespace-to-zone mapping

Zone mapping creates placement options. It does not create application availability by itself.

Namespace mapped to one zone

Use for non-production, specialised infrastructure, locality requirements, or workloads that do not require zone-level resilience.

Namespace mapped to multiple zones

Exposes several eligible placement domains and can support multi-zone VKS and workload designs.

Multi-zone availability still requires:
  • Correct VKS control-plane and worker-node topology
  • Pod replica distribution and topology controls
  • Storage topology and data accessibility
  • Load-balancer reachability
  • Application-level failure handling
Critical point

Multi-zone infrastructure provides placement options. Application availability still depends on how the cluster and application use those options.

09 · Two access-control layers

Platform permissions and Kubernetes RBAC operate at different layers.

Layer 1: vSphere Namespace access

Controls who may create or manage VKS clusters, deploy VM Service workloads, inspect namespace resources, and consume assigned capabilities.

Layer 2: VKS cluster access

Kubernetes RBAC controls actions on Kubernetes API resources through Roles, ClusterRoles, RoleBindings, and ClusterRoleBindings.

A user may be allowed to create a VKS cluster without being authorised to administer every Kubernetes namespace inside it. Likewise, a developer may have broad permissions inside one Kubernetes namespace while having no right to change the vSphere Namespace quota or zone mapping.

Memory hook

Platform access gets you the building. Kubernetes RBAC determines which rooms you may use inside the application environment.

10 · Responsibility model

The platform team creates the boundaries. Application teams must use them correctly.

Platform team

  • Supervisor and namespace lifecycle
  • Capacity and quota policy
  • Zone design and mappings
  • Storage policies and VM Classes
  • Identity integration and platform permissions
  • Approved Supervisor Services

Infrastructure teams

  • ESX cluster and zone capacity
  • Physical failure domains
  • VDS or NSX networking
  • Load balancing
  • Storage systems and policies
  • DNS, NTP, IPAM, and routing

Application teams

  • VKS cluster consumption
  • Kubernetes namespace design
  • Application RBAC
  • Workloads and Services
  • Requests and limits
  • Application availability and backup requirements

11 · Design scenario

Payments, Fraud Analytics, and Reporting should not share one platform boundary.

A financial-services company wants to provide VKS as an internal platform. Its application groups have different availability, compute, storage, and access requirements.

12 · Knowledge reinforcement

Check the boundary and placement concepts.

1. Which statement best describes a vSphere Namespace?

Correct answer: B.
  • A folder organises inventory; a vSphere Namespace actively governs consumption.
  • Kubernetes namespaces still exist inside VKS clusters.
  • A namespace is not a physical host group.

2. What is the main difference between a vSphere Namespace and a Kubernetes namespace?

Correct answer: A.

The two namespace types operate at different layers and have different owners, capabilities, and lifecycles.

3. What does mapping a vSphere Namespace to multiple zones provide?

Correct answer: B.

Multi-zone mapping exposes additional infrastructure placement domains. The cluster and application must still use them correctly.

4. Which model provides the strongest management/workload separation?

Correct answer: B.

This model separates a resilient management plane from dedicated workload infrastructure.

5. Where should application-team RBAC for Deployments and Services normally be implemented?

Correct answer: B.

Kubernetes RBAC controls access to Kubernetes API resources inside the VKS cluster.

13 · Architecture challenge

Design a regulated internal application platform.

Workload groups

  • Customer Payments production
  • Customer Payments non-production
  • Fraud Analytics with GPUs
  • Shared Reporting

Requirements

  • Production and non-production require different access groups.
  • Payments production must tolerate a zone-level infrastructure failure.
  • Fraud Analytics must use GPU-enabled infrastructure.
  • Reporting can begin in one zone.
  • Each group needs independent quota and storage policy.

Your design task: Decide the namespace count, zone mappings, VM Classes, storage policies, access groups, and remaining application-team responsibilities.

Reveal one reasonable design

This is not the only valid design. The objective is to align boundaries with ownership, risk, capacity, and availability.

14 · Key takeaways

Use the simplest boundary and zone model that meets the requirement.

  1. A vSphere Namespace is a platform resource, access, service-consumption, and placement boundary.
  2. A Kubernetes namespace scopes namespaced resources inside one Kubernetes cluster.
  3. Namespace design should follow ownership, policy, lifecycle, capacity, and risk.
  4. vSphere Zones provide infrastructure placement and failure-domain options.
  5. Management Zones and Workload Zones may be combined or isolated.
  6. Multi-zone mapping does not automatically make applications highly available.
  7. Platform RBAC and Kubernetes RBAC operate at different layers.
  8. Start simple, then add isolation and zone complexity deliberately.
