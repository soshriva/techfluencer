Harbor in VKS: More Than a Container Registry

A container registry is where images live. Harbor can be much more: the governed software supply-chain service that gives VKS teams a trusted place to publish, scan, control, replicate and consume the content that runs on their platform.

01 · Why a registry matters

Kubernetes can schedule a container only after it can obtain the image.

Every deployment begins with an image reference. But downloading an image is not the same as trusting it. Production teams need to know who published it, which version is approved, whether it has known vulnerabilities, where it is stored, and who is allowed to pull it.

Without a shared registry strategy, each team makes local choices: public registries, personal credentials, different scanning practices, inconsistent retention and unclear ownership. That works for a lab. It becomes difficult to defend in a regulated or multi-team platform.

The key idea: Harbor is not merely a place to push images. In VKS, it can be the controlled hand-off point between software delivery and workload execution.

02 · What Harbor adds

Harbor is an OCI registry with the operational controls that a shared platform needs.

At its core, Harbor stores OCI-compatible container images and artifacts. The enterprise value comes from the controls around that content: projects and role-based access, external identity integration, robot accounts for automation, vulnerability scanning, signing, replication, auditability and quotas.

For application teams

A predictable repository endpoint, project-level access and machine credentials for CI/CD. Teams can publish and pull their own approved images without operating a registry infrastructure.

For platform and security teams

A central policy and lifecycle point for image content: identity, scanning, retention, replication, capacity, monitoring and incident response can be operated consistently.

That does not mean every image must be centrally controlled in the same way. The platform can use separate Harbor projects, quotas, role bindings and policies to give teams autonomy within a defined boundary.

03 · Do not mix up the content types

Application images and VKS packages both use OCI distribution, but they serve different consumers.

ContentConsumerPurposeTypical owner
Application container imageVKS workloadRuns an application componentApplication team
VKS Standard Package / add-on contentVKS cluster or platform workflowAdds a supported Kubernetes capabilityPlatform or cluster team
Supervisor Service artifactSupervisorDelivers a VCF-governed platform serviceCloud Admin / platform team

Harbor can host private OCI content, including application images and content required by private package workflows. However, do not state that VKS packages are always stored in Harbor: VKS can use Broadcom-provided registries or another private OCI registry. In VCF 9.1, Regional Harbor is the VCF-managed option for hosting selected platform artifacts, plugins and VKS Standard Packages.

04 · Choose the delivery model deliberately

“Use Harbor” is not yet an architecture decision.

ModelWhere it runs / scopeUse it whenWatch for
Harbor in a VKS clusterInside one workload clusterA team needs a local or non-shared registry pattern.The cluster team owns availability, upgrades, storage and recovery.
Harbor as a Supervisor ServiceInstalled on the Supervisor; consumed as a governed platform capabilitySeveral teams need one supported shared registry with central lifecycle ownership.Contour, FQDN/DNS, TLS trust, persistent storage and operational ownership are prerequisites.
Regional Harbor through VCF AutomationVCF-managed regional serviceThe VCF platform needs centrally managed registry capability for VCF artifacts, VKS packages and plugins.It is a platform service; define tenant access and content-retention responsibilities explicitly.

These models are not mutually exclusive. A large organisation may use Regional Harbor for VCF platform content, a shared Harbor service for internal application images, and a local registry for a specialised disconnected environment. The choice should follow ownership, reach, resilience and compliance needs—not convenience alone.

05 · Make Harbor production-ready before making it self-service

A successful installation is not a service design.

Harbor becomes part of the path to production. Design it with the same care as any shared data-bearing platform service.

ExposureInstall Contour first for the Supervisor Service pattern. Assign a dedicated Harbor FQDN and map DNS to the Envoy ingress IP.
TrustUse TLS and establish CA trust so the Supervisor and VKS consumers can pull securely from Harbor.
PersistenceMap vSphere storage policies to StorageClasses and size PVC-backed registry, database, Redis and job-service data.
OperationsDefine capacity, monitoring, upgrade, backup, restore and incident procedures before publishing the service.

Network and identity decisions

  • Dedicated FQDN and DNS ownership
  • TLS certificate lifecycle and CA distribution
  • Ingress exposure and network policy
  • OIDC/LDAP integration and break-glass administration
  • Robot-account credential lifecycle for CI/CD

Data and resilience decisions

  • Storage policy, performance tier and capacity headroom
  • Project quotas and image-retention policy
  • Backup scope, restore runbook and restore testing
  • Replication or proxy-cache design for locality / disconnected use
  • Metrics, audit logs, alert thresholds and support ownership
Implementation detail: when a private Harbor CA is used with the Supervisor Service pattern, the Supervisor must trust that CA before vSphere Pods and VKS clusters can pull images. The course workflow appends the Harbor CA certificate to the image-fetcher-ca-bundle ConfigMap in the Supervisor kube-system namespace. Treat this as a controlled platform change and validate the supported procedure for your VCF release.
Practical warning: “Harbor is deployed as Kubernetes containers” does not mean it is stateless. Registry blobs and supporting services need persistent data, a recovery plan and ongoing capacity management.

06 · Turn the registry into a governance point, not a bottleneck

Separate what the platform operates from what teams control.

AreaPlatform / security teamApplication team
Harbor instanceInstalls, patches, monitors and backs up the shared service.Does not administer shared Harbor infrastructure.
Identity and accessIntegrates OIDC/LDAP, defines role model and reviews privileged access.Manages approved members / automation identities within its project.
Images and repositoriesDefines baseline retention, quota and vulnerability policy.Builds, tags, promotes and removes its own content responsibly.
CI/CD integrationPublishes secure patterns for robot accounts, tokens and webhooks.Stores credentials safely and uses the approved project endpoint.
RecoveryOwns Harbor service recovery and tests the platform runbook.Owns the business impact of its image retention and release process.

A sensible minimum pattern is one Harbor project per team or application boundary, least-privilege roles, separate robot accounts for automation, quotas to prevent a noisy neighbour, and a defined response to critical vulnerability findings. Scanning is valuable only when someone owns the policy decision that follows the scan.

07 · OpenShift translation

For OpenShift readers, Harbor is closest to Red Hat Quay.

OpenShift has an integrated image registry for cluster-local needs. Red Hat Quay is the closer comparison for a shared enterprise registry: it provides registry governance, security and multi-cluster patterns beyond the basic integrated registry.

VKS / VCFOpenShiftUseful translation
HarborRed Hat QuayEnterprise container registry
Harbor project + RBACQuay organisation + repository permissionsTeam separation and controlled access
Harbor replication / proxy cacheQuay geo-replication / mirroringLocality, resilience and disconnected patterns
Harbor Supervisor ServiceQuay deployed through Quay OperatorBoth are centrally delivered registry patterns, but the lifecycle mechanisms are not equivalent.

The important distinction: Harbor approximately maps to Quay as a product role. A Supervisor Service does not equal an OpenShift Operator. An Operator is a Kubernetes automation pattern. A Supervisor Service is the VCF delivery and governance model for a platform capability—and it can package an Operator.

08 · Practical example

One shared Harbor, three teams, clear boundaries.

Consider Payments, Risk Analytics and Reporting teams running VKS workloads. Each needs a private image path; the organisation needs consistent security controls and does not want three independently patched registry stacks.

Platform design

The platform team deploys Harbor as a Supervisor Service after designing Contour exposure, DNS, TLS, persistent storage, monitoring and recovery. It publishes a supported registry endpoint with a documented service level and operating model.

Consumer design

Each team receives an isolated Harbor project, project roles, a quota, separate robot accounts and an image policy. Teams retain control of release tags and CI/CD; they do not operate the shared Harbor infrastructure.

If Payments has a stricter policy, its project can require tighter access, retention and promotion controls. If the risk truly requires a separate availability or recovery boundary, then a separate Harbor instance or regional pattern may be justified. Do not assume that a project boundary alone answers every regulatory requirement.

09 · Knowledge check

Check the design thinking, not just the terminology.

1. Which statement is most accurate?

Correct: the second statement. Harbor provides capabilities; the organisation still needs to decide and operate the policy.

2. What should be completed before publishing Harbor as a production Supervisor Service?

Correct: the second statement. Installation is one step in making a supported service.

3. Are VKS packages always stored in Harbor?

Correct: the second statement. Do not turn a common design into a universal rule.

4. Which comparison is technically sound?

Correct: the second statement. Compare the product role and the lifecycle model separately.

10 · Architecture challenge

Design the image supply chain for a regulated VKS platform

Three application teams need to build and deploy images to VKS. The platform must prevent public-registry dependency in production, support a disconnected DR site, and let a small platform team operate the service safely.

Decide

  • Which deployment model fits: per-cluster, Supervisor Service or Regional Harbor?
  • What project / RBAC / robot-account pattern isolates the teams?
  • Where do DNS, TLS and CA trust get owned?
  • How will image content reach the DR site?
  • What retention, scanning and restore test will make the service credible?

Suggested direction

Use a centrally operated Harbor pattern for application content, with individual projects and quotas per team. Treat Contour exposure, FQDN/DNS, TLS/CA trust and persistent storage as platform prerequisites. Use replication or a controlled mirror for DR locality, and document recovery of both the Harbor service and the required release content. Keep platform artifacts and application images clearly separated so ownership stays understandable.

11 · Key takeaways

Build a trusted software supply chain, not just a registry endpoint.

  • Harbor is an OCI registry, but its value in VKS is the governance wrapped around image content.
  • Separate application images, VKS package content and Supervisor Service artifacts; they may share OCI technology without having the same ownership model.
  • For a shared Harbor service, design Contour exposure, DNS, TLS and CA trust, persistent storage, capacity, monitoring and recovery before enabling consumers.
  • Projects, RBAC, robot accounts, quotas and scanning policies let teams move independently without making the registry unmanaged.
  • Harbor is closest to Red Hat Quay in an OpenShift translation. Do not equate Supervisor Services with Operators.
  • Choose the Harbor delivery model by platform scope, support ownership, resilience and compliance—not by the fastest installation path.
